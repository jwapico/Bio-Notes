The **cytoskeleton** is a network of fibrous structures extending throughout the cytoplasm. It hosts a wide variety of cellular function, from providing structural support and motility, to hosting cytosolic enzymes. The cytoskeleton is dynamic and can parts can be disassembled and reassembled quickly, changing the shape of the cell to best fit its current circumstances. 

**Cell motility** generally involves a **motor protein** 'walking' along the cytoskeleton, with some sort of vesicle or protein attached to it. Cytoskeletal elements and motor proteins also interact with the plasma membrane and fibers outside the cell allowing it to move throughout its environment. The cytoskeleton also manipulates the plasma membranes shape, bending it inwards to form food vesicles and perform other **phagocytic** tasks.

There are 3 main components of the cytoskeleton: *microtubules, intermediate filaments, and microfilaments*.

# Microtubules

Microtubules are the largest structure of the cytoskeleton, and take the form of hollow tubes made up of globular proteins called tubulins. Tubulins are *dimers,* a molecule made up of two parts, in this case two [[Proteins#Polypeptides|polypeptides]], $\alpha$-tubulin and $\beta$-tubulin. Many of these dimers come together to form a microtubule. Due to the orientation of the dimers, the two ends of a microtubule differ, one side accumulates dimers easier than the other (this is called the *plus end*). These microtubules are the structures that form the shape of the cell, and act as 'track' that allow motor proteins and their cargo to traverse the cell. For example, microtubules guide vesicles from the [[Endoplasmic Reticulum|ER]] to the [[Golgi Apparatus|Golgi]], and from the Golgi to the plasma membrane. They are also involved in the separation of chromosomes during cell division. 

In animal cells, structure is largely defined by the **centrosome**, a region of microtubules near the [[Nucleus|nucleus]] or center of the cell which act as compression resisting girders. Within the centrosome is a pair of **centrioles** each compose of 9 sets of triplet microtubules arranged in a ring.

![[Pasted image 20251209175100.png]]

Additionally, some eukaryotic cells have **cilia** and **flagellum**, extracellular extensions containing microtubules. These structures have a diverse array of uses, acting as locomotive structures for movement or directing extracellular fluid. A cilium may also act as a signal receiving 'antenna', though these are usually non-motile and there are few per cell; this type of signaling appears to be crucial to brain function and embryonic development. 

![[Pasted image 20251209175844.png]]

Though differing in properties and function, motile flagella and cilia share a common structure: each has a group of microtubules sheathed in an extension of the membrane called the **basal body** connected to each other via large motor proteins called **dyneins.** The main structure of the extension consists of 9 doublets of microtubules arranged in a ring connected to 2 central microtubules in the middle via flexible cross linking proteins. 

> This is called the "9 + 2" pattern, non motile cilia lack central microtubules and thus exhibit a "9 + 0" pattern. 

The basal body of which the cilia or flagella is anchored to in the cell is structured much like a *centriole,* and in fact when an egg cell gets fertilized, the sperms basal body becomes the eggs centriole. The dyneins are attached to the outer microtubule pairs and create the bending/movement by 'walking' their 'feet' along the adjacent doublet, consuming ATP in the process. These walking movements are coordinated in such a way that they only happen on one side of the ring at a time, leading to differential forces which create rotation and movement as the structure of microtubules bends to accommodate the new connections.

# Micro (Actin) Filaments

**Microfilaments** are thin solid rods made out of a double twisted chain of globular proteins called **actin.** While they do occur as singular filaments, the chains can also be linked together to form a structural network when certain proteins bind to the chain, allowing for other filaments to connect and branch off the structure. 

While microtubules serve primarily to resist *compression*, microfilaments work under *tension.* For example, a network of these filaments (*cortical microfilaments*), form under tension just inside the plasma membrane, further supporting the cells shape. This network forms the outer cytoplasmic layer of the cell, called the **cortex,** having a gel-like, semi-solid consistency distinct from the more fluidic solution in the interior cytoplasm. 

Evolution has morphed microfilaments to a diverse set of structures serving many functions. For example, they make up the core of microvilli, delicate projections that increase the surface area of nutrient uptake in cells of animal digestive tracts. They also play a crucial role in motility; when actin filaments are layered with thicker filaments made of the motor protein **myosin**, projections in the myosin can bind to and move against the actin in such a way that produces muscle contraction. In amoeba and white blood cells, localized contractions can also propel/crawl the cell by extending and contracting small projections called **pseudopodia.** Additionally, in plant cells actin-protein interactions can contribute to **cytoplasmic streaming**, the circular flow of cytoplasm within cells that serves to distribute materials and speedup the movement of organelles.

# Intermediate Filaments

**Intermediate filaments** are larger than microfilaments, and smaller and microtubules. While microfilaments and microtubules are found in all eukaryotic cells, intermediate filaments tend to be more specialized, only being found in the cells of some animals, namely vertebrates. These, like microfilaments, are specialized for bearing tension, and are constructed from a particular molecular subunit from a family of [[Proteins|proteins]] whose members include the **keratins**. While microtubules and microfilaments are consistent in diameter and composition across all eukaryotic cells, intermediate filaments are much more diverse, and may vary in both. These filaments are particularly sturdy and thus play a role in enforcing cell shape and fixing the position of organelles. For example, the [[Nucleus|nucleus]] sits in a cage of intermediate filaments, and other intermediate filaments make up the **nuclear lamina,** informing the shape of the nuclear envelope, and thus nucleus as a whole. Intermediate filaments are more permanent than microtubules and filaments, which can be dis and reassembled in different parts of the cell dynamically. As a result, these filaments function as the permanent framework for the cell, and often persist after cell death.
