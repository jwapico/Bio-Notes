An enzyme is a molecular complex ([[Proteins|protein]] or ribozyme) that lowers the [[ATP and Cellular Work#Activation Energy|activation energy]] of a chemical process, allowing it to occur at a much higher rate than it would otherwise. They are utilized by organisms for **catalysis**, the acceleration of a chemical reaction by a substance (the enzyme; the *catalyst*) which is not consumed in the process. Enzymes catalyze reactions in intersecting **metabolic pathways** which may be **catabolic** (breaking down molecules, releasing energy), or **anabolic** (building molecules, consuming energy).

> An enzyme cannot change the $\Delta G$ - [[Free Energy|free energy]] - of a reaction, it cannot make an endergonic process exergonic; they only lower the **activation energy** of a specific chemical process. 

The molecule an enzyme acts on is referred to as its **substrate**, when the enzyme binds to its substrate(s) it forms an **enzyme-substrate complex.** The enzyme then manipulates the substrate(s) in some way yielding a chemical change, and the formation of the products.

- Form -> function. The 3 dimensional **active site** of an enzyme is highly specific to the reactants it evolved for, and is often only a small portion of the surface area of the catalyst, with the rest of the macromolecule forming the scaffolding and machinery necessary to deform and complete the reaction. Additionally, the shape of the enzyme changes subtly, wiggling through different poses and free energy configurations, existing in a dynamic equilibrium.

Most of these reactions are reversable, with directionality depending on which side has a more favorable $\Delta G$, which depends mainly on the relative concentrations of reactants and products; the net effect is always in the direction of equilibrium. 

Enzymes lower activation energy in a variety of ways:
- Active site provides a template on which the substrates come together in a particular orientation that is conducive for a reaction
- Active site stretches/manipulates the substrate towards its transition state, effectively reducing $E_A$ and the breakage of bonds
- Active site provides a microenvironment conducive to a reaction, i.e. lower pH than in a neutral cell from acidic R groups in the interior amino acids of the enzyme
- Amino acids in active site directly involving with the reaction, i.e. brief covalent bonding between substrate and amino acid sidechain

Enzymes are also effected by environmental factors such as temperature and pH, which inform the [[Chemical Bonds#Hydrogen Bonds|hydrogen]] and [[Chemical Bonds#Ionic Bonds|ionic]] bonds that determine the shape and function of the catalyst. Thus each enzyme has an optimal temperature and pH under which it transforms substrates to products at the quickest rate. 

Some enzymes require nonprotein molecules for catalytic activity, these are called **cofactors,** and may bind tightly permanently or loosely temporarily with the enzyme. Some are inorganic (i.e. metal ions), if the cofactor is organic it is referred to as a **coenzyme.**


# Regulation 

Enzymes may also be inhibited by certain chemicals, which bind the the enzyme modifying its shape to disrupt functionality. These may be permanent, with the inhibitor forming a [[Chemical Bonds#Covalent Bonds|covalent bond]] with the catalyst changing its shape, or temporary with the inhibitor mimicking the normal substrate and adhering to the active site. These mimicking inhibitors are called **competitive inhibitors** since they disrupt the reactant : free enzyme ratio *at the active site*. **Noncompetitive inhibitors** thus bind to other parts of the enzyme changing its shape and impeding activity. 

## Allosteric Regulation

**Allosteric regulation** describes any case in which a proteins ability to function at one site is disrupted by the binding of a *regulatory molecule* at another site of the protein. It may result in either the inhibition or stimulation the proteins activity. 

Most allosterically regulated enzymes consist of two or more subunits each with its own active site. Often these arrangements are exceedingly dynamic - oscillating between different chemically inert forms until an activating or inhibiting molecule binds to a regulatory, or *allosteric*, site, often located where subunits join. An activator stabilizes the complex into a form with a permanent active site under which catalysis occurs, whereas inhibitors stabilize the form to an inert configuration. Both of these may be permanent or impermanent, and interactions and concentrations of regulators can lead to sophisticated patterns in enzymatic and catabolic activity, with reactants, products, and regulators becoming players in complex feedback loops.

> A specific case of this is **feedback inhibition**, in which a metabolic pathway is halted by the inhibitory binding of an end product to an enzyme involved early in its synthesis. In complex metabolic pathways, enzymes are often located near each other in a membrane or organelle in such a way that is conducive to the proper function of these feedback pathways.

A substrate binding to an active site may trigger a shape change in the other units making them *more* catalytically active, this is termed **cooperation.** Hemoglobin is an example of this, with $O_2$ triggering a shape change inducing the bonding of more $O_2$ - this increases the affinity for hemoglobin to bind to $O_2$ where it is abundant, whereas in $O_2$ deprived tissues the release of each $O_2$ molecule decreases the affinity of the other binding sites.
