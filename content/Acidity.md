Occasionally, the hydrogen atoms of water molecules in an aqueous solution can transfer from one water molecule to the other, resulting in a positively charged hydronium ion (H3O+ or H+ by convention) and negatively charged hydroxide ion (OH-). This process is reversable and constantly occurring in the solution until it reaches a dynamic equilibrium. The concentrations of H+ and OH- are equal in pure water, but solvents (acids & bases) can be added to disrupt the ratio. 

We use the pH scale to quantify this ratio, with lower pH values denoting *higher* **acidity**, and high concentrations of H+ compared to OH-. Basic solutions on the other hand are less acidic, have a higher pH, and more OH- ions than H+ ions.

**Acids** are substances that lower the pH of a solution by donating H+ ions. Bases are substances the increase the pH by reacting with H+ ions, or donating OH- ions which then react with H+ ions to form water. There are also **weak acids** which disassociate and reassociate constantly until they reach an equilibrium, but still effect the solution in a way which lower the pH. For example:

H2CO3 <-> CHO3- + H+

In any aqueous solution at 25C, the product of molar concentrations of \[H+] and \[OH-] is always $10^-14$. In pure water, the concentrations are equal, with $10^-7$ H+ ions and $10^-7$ OH- ions. When an acid is added, the concentration of H+ ions compared to OH- ions increases to $10^-5$, for example. This means that for the solution to stay aqueous, the concentration of OH- ions must lower to $10^-12$.

The pH scale is just a representation of this property, describing the log base 10 concentration of **H+ ions.** This is why a pH of 7 is considered neutral, there are an equal proportion of H+ and OH- ions, $10^-7$.

$pH = -log[H+]$

A buffer is a substance that maintains the balance of pH by accepting H+ ions from the solution when they are in excess and donating them when they are depleted. Most buffers consist of a weak acid with its *corresponding base*.
