**Macromolecules** are **polymers** made from **monomers**. Proteins, nucleic acids, and large [[Carbohydrates|carbohydrates]] are known as **macromolecules** for their large size and consist of connected chain like molecules known as polymers. A **Polymer** is a long molecule consisting of many [[Organic Chemistry|organic molecules]] chained together through  [[Chemical Bonds#Covalent Bonds|covalent bonds]]. The building blocks of these polymers are smaller molecules called **monomers**. There are many monomers (life uses ~40-50), leading to an very large combination space for potential polymers. Despite this diversity, molecular structure and function can still be effectively grouped by class, the major ones being [[Carbohydrates|carbohydrates]], [[Lipids|lipids]], [[Proteins|proteins]], and nucleic acids. 

Although each class is made of different monomers, the chemical reactions by which the polymers are made and broken down remains largely the same. These processes are facilitated by **enzymes,** a special type of macromolecules (usually protein) that speeds up a type of chemical reaction. The reaction which connects monomers together to form a polymer is a **condensation reaction**, a reaction in which two molecules are covalently bonded to each other through the loss of a smaller molecule. If a water molecule is lost, it is known as a **dehydration reaction.** Polymers are disassembled in a process known as **hydrolysis,** in which the bond between monomers is broken by the addition of a water molecule (effectively the opposite of a dehydration reaction). 

The importance of these processes are showcased in our digestive system, where enzymes attack the large polymers found in our food, speeding up hydrolysis and breaking the polymer into a bunch of monomers. The monomers are then absorbed into our bloodstream and transported throughout the body where they can be absorbed into cells and, through dehydration reactions, be converted into energy and rebuilt into new, different polymers that are specialized for a cellular function.
