**Cellular membranes** are dynamic mosaics of [[Lipids|lipids]] and [[Proteins|proteins]] surrounding all cells and the organelles of eukaryotes. The base for a cellular membrane is a **phospholipid bilayer**. A single [[Lipids#Phospholipids|phospholipid]] is an *amphipathic* molecule, meaning it has both a polar hydrophilic side (head) and a non-polar hydrophobic side (tail). In an aqueous environment, the hydrophobic tails (made of non-polar carbon and hydrogen atoms) of adjacent phospholipids tend towards each other as this configuration helps them avoid the polar water molecules, and orients the hydrophilic heads towards the water. This self adhering property allows large 2 dimensional sheets to emerge which form the base of a **phospholipid bilayer.**
- Most membrane proteins are also *amphipathic*.

This structure is held together exclusively by these hydrophobic interactions, which are much weaker than most other [[Chemical Bonds|chemical bonds]] meaning phospholipid membranes are *not static,* but instead dynamic, acting almost as a fluid (**fluid mosaic model of plasma membrane**). As a result, individual lipids and some proteins can shift their position in the 2D matrix. This occurs very rapidly, adjacent phospholipids can switch positions ~$10^7$ times per second, meaning a phospholipid can traverse 2$\mu$m per second, which is about the average length of a bacterial cell. The proteins tend to move slower, and some may not move at all or be grouped in a long-lasting functional patch surrounded by groups of specialized lipids called *lipid rafts*. 

Some proteins also appear to move in a highly directed manner, potentially guided along a path set by [[Cytoskeleton|cytoskeletal fibers]] or via motor proteins. The 'viscosity' and other properties of the membrane are dependent on environmental conditions (e.g. temperature) and molecular composition as well.
