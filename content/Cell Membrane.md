**Cellular membranes** are dynamic mosaics of [[Lipids|lipids]] and [[Proteins|proteins]] surrounding all cells and organelles of eukaryotes. The base for a cellular membrane is a **phospholipid bilayer**. A single [[Lipids#Phospholipids|phospholipid]] is an *amphipathic* molecule, meaning it has both a polar hydrophilic side (head) and a non-polar hydrophobic side (tail). In an aqueous environment, the hydrophobic tails (made of non-polar carbon and hydrogen atoms) of adjacent phospholipids tend towards each other as this configuration helps them avoid the polar water molecules, and orients the hydrophilic heads towards the water. This self adhering property allows large 2 dimensional sheets to emerge which form the base of a **phospholipid bilayer.**
- Most membrane proteins are also *amphipathic*.

# Structure and Composition

The structure is held together exclusively by these hydrophobic interactions, which are much weaker than most other [[Chemical Bonds|chemical bonds]] meaning phospholipid membranes are *not static,* but dynamic, acting almost as a fluid (**fluid mosaic model of plasma membrane**). As a result, individual lipids and some proteins can shift their position in the 2D matrix. This occurs very rapidly, adjacent phospholipids can switch positions ~$10^7$ times per second, meaning a phospholipid can traverse 2$\mu$m per second, which is about the average length of a bacterial cell. The proteins tend to move slower, and some may not move at all or be grouped in a long-lasting functional patch surrounded by groups of specialized lipids called *lipid rafts*. 

Some proteins also appear to move in a highly directed manner, potentially guided along a path set by [[Cytoskeleton|cytoskeletal fibers]] or via motor proteins. The 'viscosity' and other properties of the membrane are dependent on environmental conditions (e.g. temperature) and molecular composition. One such permutation of molecular composition is the existence/abundance of double [[Chemical Bonds#Covalent Bonds|covalent bonds]] between carbon atoms in the (now **unsaturated**) hydrocarbon tails, which creates a kink in the structure preventing adjacent phospholipids from getting too close, making the membrane more fluid. Additionally, the [[Lipids#Steroids|steroid]] **cholesterol** may be wedged between phospholipid molecules in animal cells, this addition has different properties at different temperatures. At higher temperatures, cholesterol restrains phospholipid movement which makes the membrane more *rigid*, while at lower temperatures it hinders close packing making the membrane more *fluid*; thus, cholesterol acts as a *fluidity buffer* for the membrane, reducing the effects of changes in environmental temperature. 
- While plants don't have cholesterol, they have functionally similar steroid lipids buffering membrane fluidity.

> Membrane structure and fluidity affect its permeability and the function of membrane proteins and enzymes; as a result the composition must be fine tuned to the environment of the cell/organism. Many organisms have evolved the ability to change their lipid composition in response to environmental pressures. Plants increase the concentration of *unsaturated* phospholipids in the winter in order to maintain fluidity in the colder temperatures. 

# Transport
## Passive Transport

Small non-polar molecules such as $CO_2$ and $O_2$ can passively diffuse across the membrane since they are hydrophobic like the lipid tails. Polar molecules passively diffuse through the membrane much slower since they are unlikely to make it past the tails. Ions and polar molecules are regulated more precisely through the use of **transmembrane proteins** which form a hydrophilic channel or selectively deforming structure (**carrier proteins**) which is specialized to allow only a specific ion or molecule to pass.

> Many ion channels function as **gated channels**, which only allow the ion to pass in response to an external stimulus which may be electrical or chemical. These gated channels allow for more elaborate control and feedback loops, and are utilized heavily by the nervous system in mammals. 

Generally, the flow of a molecule across a membrane is informed by the concentration gradient of that molecule across the membrane, which holds potential energy. The diffusion of these molecules across the membrane is called **passive transport** because it does not require energy from the cell. Diffusion of ions or molecules across transmembrane proteins is called **facilitated diffusion**.  The process is called **osmosis** when the molecule is water, and is greatly increased by the presence of **aquaporins**, which are transmembrane proteins consisting of 4 polypeptides each with their own hydrophilic channel.

The diffusion of water across concentration gradients is also the mechanism behind tonicity, as water will diffuse across the cell membrane in order to relieve the gradient, which will cause the cell to shrink (give up water) or expand (take up water) depending on whether there are more non-penetrating solutes on the outside (hypertonic) or inside (hypotonic) of the cell. These dynamics are affected by the cell wall in plants, which allows for more careful regulation of water concentrations, with plant cells preferring an hypotonic environment which allows them to maintain **turgor pressure**. Organisms without cell walls evolve other methods for **osmoregulation** such as contractile vacuole pumps in protists that live in hypotonic environments.

## Active Transport

While all the transport methods above work from concentration gradients and the naturally occurring forces behind diffusion, cells may also have transmembrane proteins that consume energy to pump ions or molecules against the concentration gradient. ATP hydrolysis is the primary form of energy performing this work, as often the proteins work by binding to and breaking off the phosphate group from an ATP molecule, which causes the protein to temporarily change its shape, which can transport an ion or molecule across the membrane against the concentration gradient. 

![[Pasted image 20260126223054.png]]

The unequal distribution of ions across a membrane causes an electrical **membrane potential** that also produces forces on molecules near the membrane. Generally this potential ranges from -50 mV to -200 mV, with the negative number indicating that the *inside* of the cell is electronegative. As a result, not only are there *chemical* diffusion forces acting on molecules near the membrane, but *electric potential* forces as well. The combination of forces acting on an is called the **electrochemical gradient**. 

A transport protein that generated voltage across a membrane is called an **electrogenic pump**. An example of which is the sodium-potassium pump above, which transfers 3 $Na^+$ ions out of the cell and 2 $K^+$ ions into the cell, resulting in a net transfer of one positive charge from the cytoplasm to extracellular matrix, building up the potential and storing energy via voltage on each cycle. While this pump is the main electrogenic pump in animal cells, plants, fungi, and bacteria use the proton pump, which actively transports $H^+$ ions out of the cell.

Additionally, solutes can do work as they diffuse down their concentration gradient. **Cotransport proteins** take advantage of this by coupling the downhill diffusion of a solute with the uphill transport of a second molecule against its own concentration gradient. Plant cells do this by utilizing the gradient of $H^+$ ions generated by their proton pumps to drive the active transport of amino acids, sugars, and other nutrients into the cell. This process is used by plants to move the sugars generated by photosynthetic cells/tissues into vascular tissues that can distribute them to other parts of the plant that can't generate sugars by themselves.

![[Pasted image 20260126224914.png]]

Another form of active transport is **exocytosis**, which bundles macromolecules such as proteins and polysaccharides that are too large to diffuse across the membrane into membrane bound vesicles originating from the [[Golgi Apparatus]]. These vesicles are transported via microfilaments of the [[Cytoskeleton]] to the cell membrane, where proteins from each membrane rearrange the lipid bilayers allowing them to fuse, after which the contents of the vesicle spill out into the extracellular matrix, and the vesicle membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane. 

**Endocytosis** is generally the same process but in reverse, where the membrane sinks in forming a pocket that deepens and eventually pinches off, consuming part of the membrane that forms a new vesicle with the extracellular contents inside of it. 

![[Pasted image 20260126202307.png]]

Short, branched chains of [[carbohydrates]] may also bond to lipids or proteins in the membrane, transforming them into **glycolipids** and **glycoproteins** respectively. The carbohydrates on the extracellular side of the membrane may vary from species to species, individual to individual, or even cell to cell in the tissues of a single organism. The diversity of these structures is used in cell signaling and individuation. For example the different blood types are determined by their glycoproteins, whose characteristic structure is utilized by the immune system for recognition. The carbohydrates get built and attached to their lipids/proteins as the products move through the [[Endoplasmic Reticulum|ER]].
