Cricks academic career, that which started when he started pursuing the research *he* was interested in, began from grants wrought from equally ambitious and practical proposals:

> The particular field which excited my interest is the division between the living and the non-living, as typified by, say, proteins, virus, bacteria and the structure of chromosomes. The eventual goal, which is somewhat remote, is the description of these activities in terms of their structure, i.e., the spatial distribution of their constituent atoms, in so far as this may prove possible. This might be called the chemical physics of biology.

He then goes on to layout how he will build the intellectual framework which will allow him to pursue such an ambitious program:

> a) A general knowledge of these related sciences (biochemistry, bacteriology, genetics, etc.) sufficient to enable one to appreciate the advances and limitations of their methods, and the significance of current work.
> 
> b) A thorough grounding in at least one science. In my case this would of course be physics; but I realize that this would have to be extended in the direction of physical chemistry.

Crucially in (a) is "sufficient to enable one to appreciate the advances and *limitations* of their methods, and the *significance of current work*"

> It's worth noting that he did not initially work for the professor he first had interest in, or even begin in a Masters or PhD program - he began his first forays into molecular biology via a 'studentship for for training in research methods' under a cellular biologist. This is because the governing body handing out grants (MRC), decided he needed training in biology, that which the physics professor he wanted to work for could not provide. 
> 
> As the aforementioned cellular biologist put it: "it has been out experience that physicists in particular are very apt to over-simplify the interpretation of biological phenomena whey they first begin work in this field."

His research at this time had little impact or significance, but he felt that his "wide reading and informal attendance at lectures had a decisive impact - he later said, 'a lot of my ideas formed in that time.'"

> "Odile luxuriated in her newly found leisure, read French novels in front of the small gas fire, and attended, informally, a few lectures on French literature, while I reveled in the romance of doing real scientific research and in the fascination of my new subject."
> 
> Sounds nice

Interestingly, the structure of DNA was not Crick's formal project, it was his interest and obsession - spurred on by the social structures of the lab and crucially his friendship with Watson. On the heels of their monumental discovery, Crick gave some valuable insight on the pursuit of scientific endeavor:
- "Scientific discovery is tortuous." Chaotic. Not a march of logical steps but unpredictable and spontaneous.
- "Collaboration and personal matters" Without Watson Crick would have never started on DNA, similarly his models were inspired and confirmed by data gathered by other individuals and labs. Many inspiring and insightful conversations happened outside of the lab during social outings.
- "Preconceived ideas, plus informed thinking, plus adventurousness" corresponding to his intuition that the structure is helical, the importance of general knowledge and readiness to explore unorthodox solutions
- Making leaps is easier when you have a collaborator to tell you when you've jumped too far or when something "seems right."

> "He also encouraged the development of new research areas, each of which would require the 'right' organism - the system that could provide the best way of approaching the problem, combining manipulability and simplicity... 'the actual choice involves a deep intellectual analysis of the nature of the problem and the sort of methods likely to be required to solve it.'"

> "The search for fundamental postulates is what underpinned his scientific approach , revealing his ambition and confidence that, as a newcomer, he could understand things that the established experts in the field could not."
> 
> "'One of the mistakes embryologists have made in the past is to concentrate on the system that they happen to be interested in instead of trying to discover the simplest system which displays the properties they are interested in.'"
> 
> "Francis began to realize that there would not be a simple set of fundamental postulates that would explain development, even in one dimension. It seemed as though the detail of development *was* the bigger picture - there was little room here for Francis to set aside troublesome results."

Crick got a bit cooky and over confident a couple decades on the coasting following the discovery and resulting success (early 70s). Mainly the result of taking over confident stabs at problems with overly high solution spaces.

> This period marks a time in which he strayed from his previously highly concrete fields of inquiry into those at higher levels of complexity in which such simple seemingly elegant models cannot capture all the complexity of the system: "forgetting a Francis Bacon quote warning he himself included in an earlier paper - 'it cannot be that axioms established by argumentation can suffice for the discovery of new works, since the subtlety of nature is greater many times than the subtlety of argument.'"
