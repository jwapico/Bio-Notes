The genetic material of eukaryotic cells is housed in the **nucleus**, an organelle separated from the cytoplasm via a *double membrane* layer called the **nuclear envelope.** Both membranes are lipid bilayers with associated proteins, and throughout the envelope are pore structures in which both membranes are *continuous*. An intricate protein structure called the **pore complex** lines these pores, and regulates the passage of macromolecules, proteins, and RNA into and out of the nucleus. The inside (nuclear) side of the envelope is lined by the **nuclear lamina**, a network of protein filaments that mechanically support the nuclear envelope and inform the overall shape of the nucleus. There is also evidence for a **nuclear matrix**, a framework of protein fibers throughout entire interior of the nucleus. The *lamina and matrix* likely serve to organize and transport genetic material such that the nucleus can function efficiently. 

[[DNA]] is organized into discrete units known as **chromosomes**. Each chromosome is made up of a long strand of DNA bound and associated with a variety of proteins (such as **histones**) that help to coil and condense the string of genetic information, making it more spatially efficient. The complexes of DNA and proteins that make up chromosomes are called **chromatin.** As the cell prepares to divide, the chromatin loops and coils, bundling the information into chromosomes dense enough to (maybe) be seen under a microscope. When the cell is not dividing, the chromatin is much less dense as the genetic information must be unwound for transcription, and is visible only as a diffuse mass, though discrete chromosomes are still present. 

- Eukaryotic species have a characteristic total number of chromosomes, for example humans have 46 and flies 8. Sex cells however only include half of the genetic information, so human sex cells have 23 chromosomes and flies 4.

Another structure within the nondividing nucleus is the **nucleolus**, a mass of proteins and fibers adjoined to parts of the chromatin. Here, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is synthesized from the DNA, and with other proteins imported from the cytoplasm, is assembled into the individual subunits of **[[Ribosomes|ribosomes]]**. The subunits exit the nucleus through the nuclear pores, and assemble into an actual ribosome within the cytoplasm.

The nucleus directs protein synthesis via messenger RNA (mRNA), which is synthesized from DNA in the nucleus, transported outside through the pores, and translated by ribosomes into the primary structure of a [[Proteins|polypeptide]], which eventually morphs into a [[Proteins|protein]].
