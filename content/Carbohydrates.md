Carbohydrates are sugars and polymers of sugars. The simplest sugars are monosaccharides, which are typically multiples of the unit $CH_2O$. These elements end up forming carbonyl and hydroxyl groups, which give carbohydrates their properties.

Monosaccharides are important cellular nutrients. The cell can extract energy from the compound through a series of [[Chemical Reactions|chemical reactions]] (cellular respiration), and can also use the new constituent parts to form other organic molecules, such as amino acids and fatty acids. Monosaccharides can also be bonded to each other in many ways to form **disaccharides** and **polysaccharides**. 

A **disaccharide** consists of two monosaccharides bonded via a glycosidic linkage, formed through a dehydration reaction.

![[Pasted image 20251114172415.png]]

**Polysaccharides** are long chains of hundreds to thousands of monosaccharides joined by glycosidic linkages. The structure and function of a polysaccharide is determined by the type of monosaccharides and positions of glycosidic linkages. Generally, we divide them into two groups: storage and structural.

> Greek letters are often used as a "numbering" system for different versions of biological structures. For example, glucose rings can form in two ways depending on if the hydroxyl group attached to the #1 carbon forms above or below the ring of the plane. These two forms are alpha ($\alpha$) and beta ($\beta$) respectively.

**Storage polysaccharides** are used by the organism as an energy store. Plants use **starch**, which consists of glucose molecules forming largely unbranched helical chains. Animals store glycogen, which is more extensively branched forming a larger surface area, allowing the organism to release more glucose as the demand for energy increases. Storage polysaccharides (such as starch) typically consist of glucose molecules in the $\alpha$ form, which leads to the polymers helical structure, which stores the energy in an efficient spatial configuration. 

**Structural polysaccharides** are more rigid structures used by the organism to create form. Plants use cellulose, which like starch, consists of 1-4 glycosidic linkages. The glucose molecules of cellulose fibers have rings in the $\beta$ form, which causes the molecules to alternate orientation, giving cellulose its unbranched, straight structure. Additionally, the hydroxyl groups on the glucose monomers are free to [[Chemical Bonds#Hydrogen Bonds|hydrogen bond]] with the hydroxyls of other glucose molecules parallel to it, forming a fibrous matrix.
