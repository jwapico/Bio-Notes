While animal cells lack the structured [[The Cell Wall|cell walls]] found in plants, they have an elaborate **extracellular matrix (ECM)** that has a variety of uses from nutrient transport to signaling. The ECM consists primarily of glycoproteins and other [[Carbohydrates|carbohydrate]] containing molecules that form a fibrous network outside the cell. One of the most abundant glycoproteins found in animal cells is collagen, the fibers of which are embedded in a web of **proteoglycan** complexes. A Proteoglycan consists of a small protein with many large carbohydrates [[Chemical Bonds#Covalent Bonds|covalently bonded]] to it, and they form more structured complexes by attaching to long polysaccharides.

Cells attach to and interface with the ECM via glycoproteins such as **fibronectin,** which bind to receptors on the plasma membrane called **integrins**, which span the membrane and connect to microfilaments inside the cells [[Cytoskeleton|cytoskeleton]]. These connected structures play important roles in cell signaling, the ECM can influence cellular behavior and gene activity through the combinations of mechanical and chemical signaling that arise as these systems interact. In this way, the extracellular conditions within a tissue help coordinate and differentiate the behavior of cells within a particular region. 

Cells within an animal tissue complex have many ways of connecting to one another for structural stability and the passage of nutrients, ions, and signals.
