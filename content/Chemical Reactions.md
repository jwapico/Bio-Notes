
Chemical reactions make and break [[Chemical Bonds|chemical bonds]], changing the composition and properties of [[Fundamentals of Matter|matter]]. These reactions often change the type of bond or identity of the bond partner atoms, such as when two H2 molecules and one O2 molecule react and form two H20 molecules. The starting molecule(s) are called the **reactants**, and resulting molecule(s) are called the **products**. In reality, often a single 'chemical reaction' is actually a sequence of many smaller reactions and transformations occurring over a period of time which eventually yields the final product. Often to form larger complicated structures, energy is needed to rearrange bonds and electrons. Photosynthesis, for example, transforms six CO2 and six H2O atoms to the more complicated arrangement C6H12O6 (and 6O2) via the energy of photons in light.

Reactions cannot create or destroy atoms, only redistribute the [[Electrons|electrons]] around them - matter is always conserved. Similarly, all chemical reactions are reversible in theory, with products becoming reactants, and reactants becoming products.

In a medium in which a chemical reaction is occurring, one of the factors affecting the rate of the reaction is concentration of reactants. The more reactants there are, the faster they collide, react, and form their product. This is also true for products, as they also collide and can break back down into their reactants. During a reaction, the concentrations of reactants and products fluctuate as the molecules interact with each other in different parts of the medium. After a period of time, these fluctuations will settle down and the concentrations of reactants and products will balance out reaching a **chemical equilibrium**. This is a dynamic equilibrium though, as reactions between the different molecules are always occurring, just with no net change in concentration. This equilibrium can span a large range of ratios of reactants and products.


# Temperature and Heat

Anything that moves has **kinetic energy**. Atoms and molecules are always moving randomly within a space, the **thermal energy** of a substance is defined as the *total kinetic energy* within a volume of matter. This is different than **temperature**, which is the *average* kinetic energy in a body of matter and is independent from volume. Whenever two substances with different temperatures are brought together, the kinetic energy spreads out until it reaches an equilibrium. This is **heat**, the transfer of thermal energy from one body of matter to another, heat is a function of time. **Calories** are one way to measure heat, defined as to raise a 1 g volume of water by 1 C; similarly, 1 calorie of heat is released when a 1 g volume of water cools by 1 C. **Joules** are also used to measure heat (transfers of energy across time), one joule equals 0.239 calorie, one calorie equals 4.184 J.

Kinetic energy is also responsible for the evaporation of liquids, as molecules moving fast enough overcome the forces of its neighbors and enter the air. **Heat of vaporization** is the amount of heat 1 g of liquid must absorb for it to transition to gas. This is the mechanism behind **evaporative cooling**, which occurs because the molecules leaving the substance *carry kinetic energy with them*, releasing heat.

Similarly, when kinetic energy is released from a volume of liquid, the atoms and molecules slow down. At low enough temperatures, the force of the [[Chemical Bonds#Hydrogen Bonds|hydrogen bonds]] overcomes the kinetic energy, causing the liquid to crystallize and transform to a solid.


# Measuring Solute Concentrations in Aqueous Solutions

Mass is used to measure numbers of molecules by adding the atomic mass of each atom of each molecule. This calculation gives us a value in **daltons**, but we don't work with countable numbers of molecules in labs, so we need a way to convert this to measurable grams. To accomplish this we invented a new unit, the **mole (mol)**. A mol is defined as exactly 6.022... * 10^23 objects, this is *Avogadro's number*. Because of the way daltons and Avogadro's number were defined, there are Avogadro's number of daltons in 1 gram. This conversion scaling from molecules to human proportions of mass allows us to measure out solutes and solvents in terms of *ratios of molecules*. We do this with **molarity (M)**, which is defined as the number of moles per liter of solution.

The [[Acidity|acidity]] of an aqueous solution also informs its reactivity, with more acidic solutions having a higher concentration electrophilic H+ (H3O+) ions ready to donate the extra H atom to other molecules.
