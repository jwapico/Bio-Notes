**Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)** is an organic molecule containing the sugar ribose with the nitrogenous base adenosine and a chain of three phosphate groups - the hydrolysis of which is [[Free Energy|exergonic]] and can be utilized to perform cellular work.

> Note that the phosphate bonds are not unusually strong, but the reactants (ATP and water) have a high [[free energy]] relative to the products, so the release of energy comes from the *chemical change* of the system from higher free energy to lower free energy, not the bonds themselves. The reason ATP in particular is an effective transporter of energy is because the phosphate groups are all negatively charged (chemically analogous to a compressed spring), allowing the energy contained in this unstable arrangement to be released during hydrolysis. 

The energy released during **ATP hydrolysis** is used by the cell to perform three kinds of work:
- **Chemical work:** to drive *endergonic reactions* that would not occur spontaneously, i.e., the synthesis of polymers
- **Transport work:** the pumping of ions or other molecules against their concentration gradient
- **Mechanical work:** the beating of [[Cytoskeleton|cilia]], contraction of muscle cells, movement of chromosomes, etc.

All of these transformations are a form of **energy coupling**, the use of an exergonic process (ATP hydrolysis), to drive an endergonic process. Given that the magnitude of the negative $\Delta G$ contribution of the endergonic process is less than the positive $\Delta G$ contribution of ATP hydrolysis, the coupled reaction is exergonic and can occur spontaneously. 

These processes typically involve **phosphorylation,** the transfer of the released phosphate group from ATP to a recipient molecule (via a [[Chemical Bonds#Covalent Bonds|covalent bond]]) which becomes the **phosphorylated intermediate**. This transformation makes the intermediate less stable ($+\Delta G$), changing its shape and often ability to bind to other molecules, and thus its chemical properties. This transformative property is utilized to perform transport and mechanical work, as seen in the opening of a transport protein, or in the motor proteins driving 'walking' [[Cytoskeleton|cytoskeletal]] elements. 

> The hydrolysis and subsequent phosphorylation are of course, not perfectly efficient - also releasing heat, increasing entropy, and following the second law of [[thermodynamics]]

> ATP phosphorylation can also be used to drive chemical work as seen above

Symmetrically, ATP can be regenerated by phosphorylating ADP via a catabolic, exergonic, breakdown reaction (such as cellular respiration or photosynthesis). These cyclical transformations involving phosphate groups and energy are called the **ATP cycle,** which couples spontaneous exergonic reactions to endergonic ones used to perform cellular work. This process occurs extremely fast, with a muscle cell recycling its entire pool of ATP in less than a minute - that's 10 million molecules of ATP consumed and regenerated per second in a single cell.

# Activation Energy

Any chemical process with a $-\Delta G$  is energetically favorable and may occur spontaneously, but could take a long amount of time. This is because the **activation energy** of the reaction may be quite large; often the starting molecules in a given reaction need to contort or deform in some way before they chemical change occurs. To reach this state, the reactants may absorb energy from their surroundings and through kinematic collisions previous bonds break, then new ones form, with heat energy being released with them and the molecule in a more stable state. The energy required for this transformation to take place is called the *free energy of activation*, or simply activation energy, abbreviated $E_A$.
