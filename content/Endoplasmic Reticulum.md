The **endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** is an extensive network of membranes that houses diverse metabolic processes and ribosomes. The interior of the ER, called the *ER lumen*, is separated from the cytosol and is continuous with the space between the two layers of the nuclear envelope. There are two distinct yet connected regions of the ER, the **smooth ER** which lacks [[Ribosomes|ribosomes]], and **rough ER**, in which ribosomes are integrated within the membrane. 

The **smooth ER** performs many functions within the cell, primarily acting as a controlled region in which a diverse array of metabolic processes can take place. These processes vary by cell type, but may include the synthesis of [[Lipids|lipids]], oils, steroids, the metabolism of [[Carbohydrates|carbohydrates]], detoxification of drugs and poisons, and the storage of calcium ions. 

The **rough ER** contains many ribosomes embedded within the membrane, making it the primary site of protein synthesis within the cell. As a polypeptide chain grows from a bound ribosome, it is threaded back into the lumen through a pore formed by a protein complex in the ER membrane which helps the polypeptide fold into its functional shape. Most secretory proteins are called **glycoproteins**, proteins with carbohydrates [[Chemical Bonds#Covalent Bonds|covalently bonded]] to them. The carbohydrates are attached to the protein via specialized enzymes built into the ER membrane. After the secretory proteins are fully formed in the lumen, they can depart from the ER in a **transport vesicle** that buds off from a region called the *transitional ER*. The rough ER is also responsible for membrane production (in addition to the smooth ER), which are also transported via vesicles. The ER is responsible for its own growth, as well as the synthesis of many components within the cell, and it (as well as regions within it) is highly specialized to the cell type and the cellular components which it synthesizes.
