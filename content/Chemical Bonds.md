Different atoms can come together to form **chemical bonds** through the interactions of free [[Electrons|electrons]] in the valence shells. The cumulative forces and interactions of these stronger and weaker bonds affect not only small groups of molecules, but different parts of larger molecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, and even organism morphology, laying the foundation for the biochemistry of life.

The morphology of these bonds, dictated by where electrons accumulate around an atom or molecule, is informed by [[Molecular Shape and Function|molecular shape and function]]. Chemical bonds form and break through [[Chemical Reactions|chemical reactions]], absorbing or releasing energy in the process.


# Covalent Bonds

A **covalent bond** is the *sharing* of a pair of valence electrons by two atoms. When two atoms with free valence electrons approach each other (through electromagnetic forces between electrons in the cloud of one and protons in the nucleus of another) and get close enough for their orbitals to overlap, they can share their electrons in order to complete each others valence shell or reach a more stable state. Two or more atoms held together by covalent bonds constitute a **molecule**

Any atom that can share valence electrons has a bonding capacity corresponding to the number of covalent bonds it can form, and is usually determined by the number of electrons required to complete its valence shell. This capacity is called an atom's **valence**, and can differ depending on the combination of single and double bonds it makes. For example, phosphorous can have a valence of 3 or 5 depending on the combination of single and double bonds it makes. 

Atoms in a molecule attract shared bonding electrons to varying degrees depending on the element and amount of protons in its nucleus. This attraction of electrons from a covalent bond to a particular atom determines the molecules **electronegativity**. The more electronegative it is, the harder it pulls the shared electrons towards its nucleus. 

In a covalent bond between atoms of the same element, the number of protons are the same, and the electrons are shared equally so the atoms have the same electronegativity. This type of covalent bond is called a **nonpolar covalent bond**, in which there are no partial charges on different side of the molecule. In a **polar covalent bond**, electrons are concentrated in such a way that different potentials arise on different sides of the atom. This is due to the atoms have differing numbers of protons in their nucleus, causing the electrons to be pulled toward the direction of the more positive atomic nucleus. Polar covalent bonds are responsible for many important structures in the biochemistry of life, and give rise to [[#Hydrogen Bonds|hydrogen bonds]].

# Ionic Bonds

In some cases, two atoms are so unequal in their electronegativity that the more electronegative atom strips an electron away from the other altogether. The two resulting oppositely charged atoms (or molecules) are called **ions**. A positively charged ion is called a **cation**, while a negatively charged ion is called an **anion**. Because of their opposite charges, cations and anions attract, this attractions forms an **ionic bond**. Understand it is not the transfer of the electron it self that forms the bond, but the attraction of the resulting oppositely charged ions that forms the bond. This means any two ions of opposite charge can form an ionic bond, they did not have to transfer electrons directly.

![[Pasted image 20251015213517.png]]

Compounds formed by ionic bonds are called **ionic compounds** or **salts**. Salts are often found in nature as crystals, each crystal is an aggregate of many cations and anions bonded by their electrical attraction in a three dimensional lattice. This means ionic compounds can grow arbitrarily large depending on the amount of oppositely charged ions, unlike covalent compounds, which consist of molecules having a definite size and number of atoms. *An ionic compound does not consist of molecules*. The formula for an ionic compound only defines the *ratios* of different atoms, not the specific amount. 

- The term *ion* also applies to entire molecules that have an electrical charge, not just single atoms. 

Environment also plays a role in ionic bond strength. Dry salt crystals may have very strong bonds, requiring lots of energy to break, but if the crystal is dissolved in water the bonds are much weaker because of their interactions with the polar water molecules. When an ionic compound is exposed to an aqueous solution, the water molecules surround each crystal of the salt, attracted to each other through through their opposite charges. The water bonding to the compound eventually causes it to break up, dissolving each ion, working in from the surface of the crystal. 

A compound does not have to be ionic to be soluble by water though. Compounds made of non-ionic polar molecules, and even proteins, can dissolve in water as long as they have large enough polar regions on their surface for the **hydrogen bonds** of water molecules to be attracted to. 

# Hydrogen Bonds

**Hydrogen bonds** are a result of the weak forces originating from the slight positive and negative charges which occur on different sides of the atomic nucleus where the concentrations of [[Electrons|electrons]] is different/unequal due to the attraction of electrons to the more positive nucleus.

![[Pasted image 20251015215419.png]]

This property is responsible for the many important characteristics of water (H2O) and other liquids. In water, these forces are ~1/20 of a covalent bond. These forces are what cause water to keep its shape, the hydrogen bonds hold the substance together, a phenomenon called **cohesion.** Hydrogen bonds are also behind **surface tension,** which is even stronger because an ordered arrangement of water molecules forms on the water air barrier.

![[Pasted image 20251025203830.png]]

Cohesion is also responsible for moving water and dissolved nutrients up the vascular tissue a plant during evaporation, as when the water is evaporated more molecules come to take its place and pulls the others near it up slightly. **Adhesion** also helps during this process, which is a result of the forces of hydrogen bonds from the molecules of cell walls, which helps resist the gravity.

Hydrogen bonds also responsible for waters high [[Chemical Reactions#Temperature and Heat|relative heat]], since the bonds resist change, absorbing energy when breaking, and releasing energy when forming. This is also the case in waters very high [[Chemical Reactions#Temperature and Heat|heat of vaporization]]. These properties of water are crucial to temperature moderation on all scales of biology: allowing oceans and lakes to act as massive heat banks regulating temperature fluctuations on land, preventing overheating in organisms, and informing biochemistry. Another important property of water is dependent on hydrogen bonds - the fact its frozen form floats on its liquid form. When water freezes, the hydrogen bonds in the water molecules don't break, and crystallize into a form 10% less dense than an equal volume of liquid water.


# Van der Waals Interactions

Van der Waals forces are weak, distance dependent electrical forces from random fluctuations of electrons accumulating on one side or area of a molecule. They occur only when atoms and molecules are very close together, but many occurring simultaneously can accumulate significant force. Specialized organisms such as geckos with pads formed of extremely fine hairs to maximize surface area utilize these forces to be able to cling to surfaces.
