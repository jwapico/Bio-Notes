The **cell wall** is a thick extracellular structure consisting of microfibrils made out of **cellulose** found in plants, fungi, prokaryotes, and some protists. The cellulose is synthesized by the enzyme cellulose synthase and secreted into the extracellular space near the cell, where it gets embedded in a matrix of other [[Carbohydrates|polysaccharides]] and [[Proteins|proteins]] making up the outer layer of the cell wall. The exact chemical composition of the cell wall differs from plant to plant, and even from cell to cell within a species. 

A young plant cell first forms a flexible and thin cell wall called the **primary cell wall**. The space in between adjacent cells is called the **middle lamella**, and is filled with sticky polysaccharides called *pectins* that serve to glue adjacent cells together. Some plant cells also produce a **secondary cell wall** between the plasma membrane and primary wall that is often generated in multiple laminated layers, making it stronger and more durable, offering protection and support. These are the primary structures that make up wood. 

Plant cells may also be perforated with **plasmodesmata,** small channels connecting adjacent cells which are lined with plasma membrane and through which cytosol and small solutes can flow freely. This unifies most of the plant into a living continuum. Experiments have also found that small proteins and RNA can pass through plasmodesmata as well, and macromolecules transported to neighboring cells arrive at the plasmodesmata by moving along fibers in the [[Cytoskeleton|cytoskeleton]].
