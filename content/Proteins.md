A **polypeptide** is an unbranched [[Polymers and Macromolecules|polymer]] made up of **amino acids**, bonded through *peptide bonds*. A **protein** is a biological molecule consisting of one or more polypeptides folded into a 3-dimensional structure.


## Amino Acids

An **amino acid** consists of a carbon atom (referred to as the $\alpha$ carbon) bonded to an H atom, a carboxyl group, an amino group, and a side chain (or R group). 

![[Pasted image 20251115173258.png]]

The R group is what defines an amino acids properties, it may be as simple as an H atom or it may be a carbon skeleton with many [[Organic Chemistry|functional groups]]. All proteins are constructed from the same set of 20 amino acids, each of which having some unique structure with polar/non-polar regions which can be leveraged by evolution serve some biological function.

![[Pasted image 20251115173335.png]]

The grouping of amino acids is determined by the properties of their side chains (R group). [[Acidity|Acidic]] amino acids have side chains that are generally negative in charge, and others have generally positively charged side chains. Due to their polar nature, amino acids are also hydrophilic.


## Polypeptides

Amino acids form polypeptides via dehydration reactions which bond the carboxyl group of one amino acid to the amino group of another, forming a [[Chemical Bonds#Covalent Bonds|covalent bond]] called a **peptide bond**. This structure of linked carboxyl and amino groups is called the *polypeptide backbone*, with various R groups extending off of it, and a free carboxyl group at one end and amino group at the other. 

![[Pasted image 20251115175415.png]]

The kind and sequence of the R group side chains determine how the protein forms and folds, thus determining its final 3-dimensional shape.


## Proteins

A **protein** is one or more polypeptides precisely twisted, coiled, and folded into a 3 dimensional shape that gives it its functional properties. These emergent forms are the result of polarity properties in the amino acid side chains, with molecules in some R groups forming [[Chemical Bonds|bonds]] with others.

![[Pasted image 20251119181512.png]]

This shape arises from 4 levels of structure:

**Primary structure:** The sequence of [[#Amino Acids|amino acids]], chain can be arbitrarily long. Determined not by random linking, but by genetic information. This structure dictates secondary and tertiary. 

**Secondary structure:** Is defined by [[Chemical Bonds#Hydrogen Bonds|hydrogen bonds]] stabilizing certain regions of the polypeptide backbone. These bonds can form helical structures ($\alpha$ helix), sheet structures ($\beta$ pleated sheet) and more.

![[Pasted image 20251119183614.png]]

**Tertiary structure:** A polypeptides tertiary structure (final 3D form) is the result of the summation of a diverse set of molecular forces from interactions between amino acid side chains. These may be [[Chemical Bonds#Hydrogen Bonds|hydrogen bonds]], [[Chemical Bonds#Ionic Bonds|ionic bonds]], [[Chemical Bonds#Van der Waals Interactions|Van der Waals interactions]] **hydrophobic interactions** (the tendency for non polar regions to be located inside the protein, away from the outside aqueous solution), or even non amino acid scaffolding called **disulfide bridges.** These form when two cystine monomers containing sulfhydryl groups (-SH) are to made to be adjacent via the folding of the protein, and a [[Chemical Bonds#Covalent Bonds|covalent bond]] is formed between the sulfur atoms. 

![[Pasted image 20251119185058.png]]

All of these interactions contribute to the 3 dimensional, tertiary structure of the protein. 

**Quaternary structure:** This is the result of two or more individual polypeptides binding together to form a much larger structure. 

![[Pasted image 20251119183914.png]]

One example is collagen, 3 identical helical subunits intertwining into a larger, much stronger helical structure.

![[Pasted image 20251119185707.png]]

And hemoglobin, which consists of alternating pairs of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ subunits.

![[Pasted image 20251119185755.png]]

The process in which this structure arises can be just as complex as the structure itself, many proteins undergo many intermediate structures on their way to their final form. This makes it very difficult to correlate primary structure with tertiary structure. Additionally, there is an entire class of proteins whose shape is not determined until they interact with their target protein or molecule. These are called *intrinsically disordered proteins,* and their flexibility and indefinite structure is important for their function, but makes them much more difficult to study and characterize. 

### Denaturation

This structure forms while the protein is being synthesized within the cell and is reliant on a certain set of environmental conditions for the delicate structure to persist. If the pH, salt concentration, solvent polarity, and temperature is altered too drastically, the weak chemical bonds holding the protein together may break, causing the protein to unravel and lose its shape, this process is called **denaturation.**
