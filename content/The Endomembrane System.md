The genetic material of eukaryotic cells is housed in the **nucleus**, an organelle separated from the cytoplasm via a *double membrane* layer called the **nuclear envelope.** Both membranes are lipid bilayers with associated proteins, and throughout the envelope are pore structures in which both membranes are *continuous*. An intricate protein structure called the **pore complex** lines these pores, and regulates the passage of macromolecules, proteins, and RNA into and out of the nucleus. The inside (nuclear) side of the envelope is lined by the **nuclear lamina**, a network of protein filaments that mechanically support the nuclear envelope and inform the overall shape of the nucleus. There is also evidence for a **nuclear matrix**, a framework of protein fibers throughout entire interior of the nucleus. The *lamina and matrix* likely serve to organize and transport genetic material such that the nucleus can function efficiently. 

[[DNA]] is organized into discrete units known as **chromosomes**.Each chromosome is made up of a single long strand of DNA bound and associated with a variety of proteins (such as **histones**) that help to coil and condense the string of genetic information, making it more spatially efficient. The complex of DNA and proteins that make up a chromosome is called **chromatin.** As the cell prepares to divide, the chromatin loops and coils, bundling the information into chromosomes potentially thick enough to be seen under a microscope. When the cell is not dividing, the chromatin is much less dense as the genetic information must be unwound for transcription, and is visible only as a diffuse mass, though discrete chromosomes are still present.
