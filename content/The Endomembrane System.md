The **endomembrane system** is a network of membrane bound organelles responsible for protein synthesis and transport, lipid metabolism and transport, and the detoxification of harmful molecules. The membranes of this system are related either through direct physical continuity or by the transport of vesicle bound products, and take on diverse forms, functions, and compositions that may evolve over time.

# Nucleus

The genetic material of eukaryotic cells is housed in the **nucleus**, an organelle separated from the cytoplasm via a *double membrane* layer called the **nuclear envelope.** Both membranes are lipid bilayers with associated proteins, and throughout the envelope are pore structures in which both membranes are *continuous*. An intricate protein structure called the **pore complex** lines these pores, and regulates the passage of macromolecules, proteins, and RNA into and out of the nucleus. The inside (nuclear) side of the envelope is lined by the **nuclear lamina**, a network of protein filaments that mechanically support the nuclear envelope and inform the overall shape of the nucleus. There is also evidence for a **nuclear matrix**, a framework of protein fibers throughout entire interior of the nucleus. The *lamina and matrix* likely serve to organize and transport genetic material such that the nucleus can function efficiently. 

[[DNA]] is organized into discrete units known as **chromosomes**. Each chromosome is made up of a long strand of DNA bound and associated with a variety of proteins (such as **histones**) that help to coil and condense the string of genetic information, making it more spatially efficient. The complexes of DNA and proteins that make up chromosomes are called **chromatin.** As the cell prepares to divide, the chromatin loops and coils, bundling the information into chromosomes dense enough to (maybe) be seen under a microscope. When the cell is not dividing, the chromatin is much less dense as the genetic information must be unwound for transcription, and is visible only as a diffuse mass, though discrete chromosomes are still present. 

- Eukaryotic species have a characteristic total number of chromosomes, for example humans have 46 and flies 8. Sex cells however only include half of the genetic information, so human sex cells have 23 chromosomes and flies 4.

Another structure within the nondividing nucleus is the **nucleolus**, a mass of proteins and fibers adjoined to parts of the chromatin. Here, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is synthesized from the DNA, and with other proteins imported from the cytoplasm, is assembled into the individual subunits of **ribosomes**. The subunits exit the nucleus through the nuclear pores, and assemble into an actual ribosome within the cytoplasm.

The nucleus directs protein synthesis via messenger RNA (mRNA), which is synthesized from DNA in the nucleus, transported outside through the pores, and translated by ribosomes into the primary structure of a [[Proteins|polypeptide]], which eventually morphs into a [[Proteins|protein]]. 


# Ribosomes

Ribosomes can conduct protein synthesis within the cytosol of the cell or bound to a membrane. *Free ribosomes* float freely in the cytosol and generally produce proteins/enzymes that float freely in the cytosol, and *bound ribosomes* are attached to the endoplasmic reticulum or nuclear envelope, and typically produce membrane bound proteins. Bound and free ribosomes are structurally identical, and can be found at either location at different times. 


# Endoplasmic Reticulum

The **endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** is an extensive network of membranes that houses diverse metabolic processes and ribosomes. The interior of the ER, called the *ER lumen*, is separated from the cytosol and is continuous with the space between the two layers of the nuclear envelope. There are two distinct yet connected regions of the ER, the **smooth ER** which lacks ribosomes, and **rough ER**, in which ribosomes are integrated within the membrane. 

The **smooth ER** performs many functions within the cell, primarily acting as a controlled region in which a diverse array of metabolic processes can take place. These processes vary by cell type, but may include the synthesis of [[Lipids|lipids]], oils, steroids, the metabolism of [[Carbohydrates|carbohydrates]], detoxification of drugs and poisons, and the storage of calcium ions. 

The **rough ER** contains many ribosomes embedded within the membrane, making it the primary site of protein synthesis within the cell. As a polypeptide chain grows from a bound ribosome, it is threaded back into the lumen through a pore formed by a protein complex in the ER membrane which helps the polypeptide fold into its functional shape. Most secretory proteins are called **glycoproteins**, proteins with carbohydrates [[Chemical Bonds#Covalent Bonds|covalently bonded]] to them. The carbohydrates are attached to the protein via specialized enzymes built into the ER membrane. After the secretory proteins are fully formed in the lumen, they can depart from the ER in a **transport vesicle** that buds off from a region called the *transitional ER*. The rough ER is also responsible for membrane production (in addition to the smooth ER), which are also transported via vesicles. The ER is responsible for its own growth, as well as the synthesis of many components within the cell, and it (as well as regions within it) is highly specialized to the cell type and the cellular components which it synthesizes.

# Golgi Apparatus

Many of the transport vesicles produced by the ER are received by the **Golgi apparatus**, which further modifies, tags, and repackages the products for transport within the cell. Physically, the Golgi consists of a group of associated, flat membranous sacs (**cisternae**), which have a structural directionality, with membranes of cisternae in different regions exhibiting distinct structure and molecular composition. The two sides are referred to as the *cis* and *trans* face, acting as the shipping and receiving areas, respectively. The *cis* face is logically located near the ER, where vesicles are transporting materials to be fused with the Golgi for further processing. On the other side, the *trans* face forms and pinches off new vesicles containing the modified, final form of the protein or macromolecule to be transported somewhere else within the cell. As the molecular products traverse the Golgi from the *cis* to *trans* side, they undergo many transformations finalizing their form. Different parts of the Golgi have distinct specialized enzymes which transform the products as they progress through the structure, making the Golgi somewhat analogous to a manufacturing line. The Golgi also sorts and tags the vesicles for transport to specific parts of the cell by adding molecular identification tags such as phosphate groups or binding sites. Many of the transport vesicles released by the Golgi are eventually incorporated into the plasma membrane, increasing its size and dispersing the rest of vesicles contents into the cytosol.

# Lysosomes

**Lysosomes** are membrane bound sacs of *hydrolytic* enzymes that are used to digest (hydrolyze) macromolecules into smaller subunits suitable for cellular use. The hydrolytic enzymes work most efficiently in an acidic environment, which is sustained within the lysosomal membrane and kept separated from the more neutral environment found within the cytosol.  Lysosomes perform digestion in a variety of ways. One of which is by fusing with a food vacuole or smaller organism which was engulfed via **phagocytosis** and mixing with and breaking down its contents. The digestive products such as simple sugars, amino acids, and other monomers then pass into the cytosol for use as nutrients within the cell. Lysosomes are also responsible for recycling the cells own organic material through a process called **autophagy**. In this process, a damaged organelle or volume of cytosol is surrounded by a double membrane effectively forming a new vesicle which the lysosome fuses to via the outer membrane, allowing its enzymes to dissolve the inner membrane and its contents.

# Vacuoles

**Vacuoles** are effectively large vesicles with selective membranes that control the contents of the containing solution, separating it from the cytosol. This functionality lends to a variety of functional uses within a cell. One type of vacuole is the *food vacuole*, which contains cellular nutrients extracted via the lysosomes. Unicellular protists have *contractile vacuoles* which allow it to pump excess water out of the cell and maintain internal ionic and molecular concentrations. Plant and fungal vacuoles exhibit particularly diverse functionality, carrying out enzymatic hydrolysis (similar to that seen in lysosomes), storing organic compounds, poisonous or unpalatable compounds serving to protect against predators, and pigments that can attract pollinators. Additionally, plant cells generally contain a large **central vacuole**, which stores 'cell sap', the cells main repository for inorganic compounds such as potassium and chloride. This central vacuole also plays an important role in plant growth, enlarging as the cell uptakes more water, physically increasing its size with minimal investment in new cytoplasm.
